# HCA200 Chapter 6 — Answer Key and Rationales

One point per correct answer; maximum 150 points.

## 1. A. Examine personal assumptions, compare intentions with effects, and plan a change to evaluate.

Reflection examines the self and contributing factors and uses insight to guide future practice. Rehearsing blame, proving oneself right, or stopping at emotion does not complete that learning process.

## 2. D. The in-the-moment examination illustrates reflexivity, complemented by later reflection.

The chapter highlights simultaneously examining oneself and interacting as an aspect of reflexivity. Later reflection can consolidate learning. Reflecting a client’s feelings back is a different communication process.

## 3. D. Considering whether the HCA’s own discomfort with sadness led them to end the visit abruptly.

Reflecting perceived feelings back to a client differs from examining one’s own motives, reactions, and contribution.

## 4. D. The account omits analysis of meaning, assumptions, contributing factors, and future responses.

Description is a foundation, not the whole reflective process. Analysis and resulting learning give the account practical value.

## 5. B. Evaluate both the overall outcome and individual events; retain helpful actions while examining avoidable interruptions.

The chapter considers the overall result while also exploring details and reactions. A positive endpoint does not remove the need to examine potentially harmful communication.

## 6. A. Establish a goal for future similar interactions.

In the seven-step reflective sequence, analysis informs future goals; strategies and action plans follow those goals.

## 7. C. Allocate time; choose an experience; describe and explore it; examine reactions and results; establish goals; develop a plan; apply learning in practice.

This preserves the course sequence: time, experience, description, exploration of reactions/results, goals, plan, and implementation. Further experience feeds the cycle. Labels are paraphrased rather than presented as verbatim diagram text.

## 8. C. Improved participation is the intended outcome; asking about order is a strategy; reviewing encounters supports evaluation.

The outcome sought, the action intended, and the later evaluation serve different functions. This makes learning actionable rather than merely aspirational.

## 9. C. Alternative actions, why they were not chosen, and their possible consequences.

This considers an alternative, the barrier to selecting it, and its likely consequences. Several elements can overlap, but this is the explicit focus.

## 10. C. I interrupted because I wanted to finish quickly and reassure myself that I was efficient.

Aims concern what the worker was trying to achieve and reasons for acting. Silence is a consequence, noise is background, and a future pause is learning or planning.

## 11. D. Resultant learning and how future reactions may change.

Johns’s final element connects the examination to learning and future reactions. The model seeks depth, not completion of a checklist alone.

## 12. B. The new event may have changed her feelings, expectations, or perceived ability to help; clarify these rather than assuming motives.

The chapter’s family-meeting example highlights how an unexpected event can alter emotions and intentions. The explanation remains a hypothesis to explore, not an established motive or placement decision.

## 13. B. Recognize the need for appreciation, seek appropriate support outside client relationships, and examine its effect on care.

The chapter warns against centring care on fulfilment of the worker’s dominant personal needs. Needs can be acknowledged and supported without making clients responsible for meeting them.

## 14. A. Ask what support, if any, the client wants, consider suitable alternatives within the care plan, and report/document relevant facts under workplace procedures.

Clarification supports choice without coercion. Relevant communication and documentation support continuity. This educational scenario does not establish a blanket legal rule or authorize independent changes outside the HCA’s role.

## 15. C. What evidence supports my interpretation, and how might my need to feel useful be shaping it?

Separating observed refusal from personal interpretation reveals assumptions and emotional needs. A refusal of assistance does not establish rejection of the worker.

## 16. B. 08:20: Client stated, “No shower now; I’m cold.” Offered to return later; client agreed. Supervising nurse informed at 08:25.

The entry uses a relevant quote, records the response and notification, and avoids inferred motives or character labels. Actual charting must also follow workplace requirements.

## 17. C. Use an authorized private setting and share only information needed for the professional discussion, following policy.

Purpose alone does not make disclosure appropriate. A private authorized discussion and limited relevant detail support confidentiality. Removing a name may not sufficiently de-identify a distinctive story.

## 18. C. Sublimation.

Sublimation channels an unacceptable impulse into a personally and socially acceptable activity. Displacement redirects emotion unhealthily toward another target.

## 19. A. Suppression.

Suppression involves temporarily setting a thought or feeling aside and returning to it. Awareness and intentional postponement distinguish it from unconscious repression or refusal to accept reality.

## 20. B. Anticipation.

Anticipation involves preparing for expected future difficulty. It differs from withdrawing into an imaginary resolution or using detail to avoid feeling.

## 21. D. Altruism.

Altruism involves selfless concern for others. An emotional benefit to the helper does not by itself make the act approval-seeking. The scenario explicitly excludes an expectation of praise.

## 22. C. Making a joke that acknowledges a shared difficulty and eases tension without dismissing pain.

Mature humour permits expression and reduces tension while recognizing seriousness. Dismissal, humiliation, and inappropriate disclosure are not justified by calling them humour.

## 23. C. Displacement.

The strong feelings are redirected unhealthily from the original person to another. The aggression has not been transformed into an acceptable outlet.

## 24. B. Projection.

Projection attributes one’s unacceptable feeling to another person. Displacement instead changes the target of the expressed feeling. These are teaching patterns, not diagnoses from a single observation.

## 25. A. Repression is an unconscious removal from awareness; suppression is a temporary decision to put something aside.

The defining distinction is awareness and temporary postponement versus unconscious exclusion. Directing anger toward another target describes displacement.

## 26. A. Reaction formation.

Reaction formation involves consciously experiencing the opposite of underlying unconscious wishes or emotions. Merely behaving politely while knowingly angry would not establish it.

## 27. D. Isolation of affect / intellectualisation.

The chapter groups isolation of affect with intellectualisation and describes using conscious detail to avoid uncomfortable emotion. Accurate practical planning alone would not establish this mechanism.

## 28. B. Denial can temporarily help functioning; persistence and consequences matter when considering its effects.

The chapter describes short-term denial as potentially helpful in grief and warns about over-reliance. Its category labels are not sufficient grounds for diagnosing a person.

## 29. B. Recognize the feeling, choose a safe response, set a respectful limit, and obtain appropriate help if safety requires it.

Self-awareness concerns managing one’s response, not accepting abuse or ignoring safety. The instructor’s example supports a respectful limit and appropriate support rather than retaliation.

## 30. D. Responsibility for managing our responses does not remove our duty to examine how our conduct affects others.

The course emphasis on personal emotional responsibility must not erase the impact of communication. Reflection examines both intentions and effects without claiming total control of another person’s feelings.

## 31. A. Explore the association and its effect on interpretation, then plan how to keep care responsive to this client.

Past experience can shape present reactions. Recognizing that influence allows the HCA to question assumptions rather than transfer an old relationship onto the current client.

## 32. D. Compare specific examples with personal observations, examine defensiveness, and identify useful changes.

Feedback is information to examine honestly, not automatically accept or reject. Specific evidence supports learning about strengths and areas for improvement.

## 33. B. A model can be selected for fit with the individual’s thinking and learning needs while retaining depth and application.

The chapter supports selecting a suitable model. Its purpose is to guide thoughtful exploration, not replace judgment, evidence, or ethical consideration.

## 34. B. Explore personal expectations and relevant context, and clarify meaning without assuming a cultural explanation.

Critical self-awareness challenges the worker’s interpretation. Context and the individual’s perspective should be explored; a cultural stereotype would simply replace one unsupported assumption with another.

## 35. D. Seek appropriate supervision or referral and use the learning to guide future communication within the role.

The chapter recognizes that no professional has every answer and that appropriate support or referral may be needed. Insight into limitations is a useful reflective outcome.

## 36. C. The worker identifies a recurring trigger, applies a justified change, and reviews its effects across later encounters.

Reflective practice involves using learning to change or reinforce behaviour over time. Label recall and volume of writing do not establish application, and discomfort can still occur.

## 37. C. Acknowledge the preference, notify the supervisor, and explore available options within staffing and policy.

Acknowledge the stated preference without treating it as a judgment of personal worth. Staffing arrangements require appropriate coordination; the HCA should neither guarantee an unavailable option nor assign an unsupported motive.

## 38. C. Would you prefer a different time, or is something about the shower uncomfortable?

The question opens discussion of timing and discomfort without presuming a motive. Leading, accusatory, and speculative questions undermine clarification.

## 39. C. Yesterday’s experience may contribute to today’s refusal; ask what would help before assuming that is the only reason.

A past experience can inform exploration without proving an exclusive cause. Reflection considers multiple factors and checks interpretations.

## 40. C. The HCA’s assumption that one schedule suits everyone needs reconsideration.

The specific learning concerns an assumption about timing and individual needs. It supports coordinated review without a guaranteed outcome or unsupported legal conclusion.

## 41. B. Disability alone does not establish incapacity; report relevant concerns for appropriate assessment and follow authorized processes.

The brief explicitly distinguishes disability from incapacity. Concerns about decision-making require appropriate professionals and processes; neither capacity nor incapacity should be inferred solely from refusal.

## 42. D. Clarify the situation, communicate relevant concerns within scope, and follow the care plan and reporting process without assuming authority to confiscate.

Respect for choice coexists with appropriate communication, reporting, and the care plan. The scenario does not grant authority to confiscate or justify involving an unrelated client.

## 43. B. Acknowledge the reported pain, report the refusal and concern promptly through the appropriate channel, and follow the plan within scope.

The concern warrants communication and appropriate escalation, including the pain barrier. Possible complications are not inevitable outcomes, and refusal alone does not diagnose infection.

## 44. C. Learn relevant information through approved education and supervision so observations and concerns can be communicated more effectively.

The instructor used reflection to identify wound-care learning needs. Education should improve practice within role, not become a basis for independent treatment decisions or coercion.

## 45. D. I should identify my anxiety, communicate relevant concerns appropriately, and avoid using fear to pressure the client.

Managing anxiety supports clear communication. It neither establishes the right decision nor replaces reporting or authorized procedures.

## 46. C. Record the observed refusal, relevant client words, what was offered or done, and notifications without claiming an assessment outside the HCA’s role.

Objective recording captures the encounter without asserting an independent capacity or informed-consent assessment that was not established. Calmness does not make relevant facts unimportant.

## 47. A. Recognize the appeal to the HCA’s emotional needs and seek guidance on maintaining the professional relationship and appropriate supports.

Familiarity and feeling needed can blur boundaries. Acknowledge the need for connection and seek appropriate support without converting the relationship into a private arrangement.

## 48. B. The worker may be allowing a personal need for approval to shape care priorities.

## 49. C. Support the client’s appropriate independence and find healthy ways to understand personal worth beyond being needed.

The worker’s sense of usefulness should not displace client goals. Reflection can reveal the need and support a different understanding of personal value.

## 50. A. Recognize how the urge could shape the interaction and adjust tone in the moment while following safety procedures.

Reflexivity includes examining personal influence while interacting. A calmer response can help but does not guarantee safety or replace procedures.

## 51. C. It can be a deliberate communication strategy; the action alone does not establish an unconscious defence mechanism.

The brief distinguishes conscious strategies from automatic assignment of defence labels. An observed tone alone does not establish the internal process.

## 52. D. A practical safety precaution.

The action is a deliberate precaution. It should not be labelled an unconscious defence mechanism merely because it helps the worker feel prepared.

## 53. C. Follow workplace procedures for obtaining help and safely withdrawing, report the situation, and later examine relevant warning signs.

Immediate safety procedures take priority; reflection can follow to support learning. It does not replace assistance or authorize retaliation.
